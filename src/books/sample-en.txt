The Lighthouse at Quiet Point

Chapter 1. The Letter

Oleg came to Quiet Point at the end of September, when the last holidaymakers had already gone home and the wind had begun to smell of rain. In his backpack he carried two sweaters, a thermos and an envelope he had not dared to open. His mother had handed it to him before he left. "Read it at the lighthouse," she had said, and she had not explained.

The road from the bus stop ran along the shore. Gray rocks stretched out on the right, and low grass bent by the wind on the left. The lighthouse did not show itself at once: first a white stripe appeared above the hill, then a red roof, and only at the gate did he understand how tall it was.

The keeper's name was Tamara. She opened the door, wiped her hands on her apron and said the kettle was already on and that she had heated the room upstairs that morning. She asked nothing more, and Oleg was grateful to her for it.

In the evening he sat down by the window. Down below, the waves moved slowly, as if they too were in no hurry. The envelope lay on the table. Oleg turned it over in his hands, put it back and decided that the morning was wiser than the evening.

Chapter 2. The Lamp

By morning it turned out that the lighthouse had not truly shone for a long time. An automatic beacon on the neighboring rock had replaced it many years ago, and Tamara kept the old lamp the way people keep a watch left behind by a grandfather. Once a week she climbed to the top, polished the lens and checked that the mechanism had not rusted.

"Would you like to see it?" she asked.

Oleg would. The spiral staircase was longer than it looked from below, and on the hundred and thirtieth step he lost count. Up at the top it was bright and quiet. The lens, built from dozens of glass rings, caught the pale morning sun and scattered it over the walls in tiny rainbows.

"My father used to say the lamp should burn at least once a year," said Tamara. "Otherwise it gets offended."

"Lamps don't get offended," Oleg said.

"This one does."

He laughed, for the first time in weeks. Then he asked whether they could light it that day. Tamara thought it over, adjusted her scarf and nodded: they only had to wash the glass before dark.

They spent the whole day washing glass, tightening screws and oiling gears. By evening Oleg's arms ached, and his head felt unusually empty and light.

Chapter 3. The Light

When darkness fell, they lit the lamp. At first it only hummed softly, then yellow stripes ran across the walls, and suddenly a beam went out, long and steady, and lay down on the water. Far below, the crests of the waves flashed.

"It works," Oleg breathed.

"Of course it works," said Tamara. "It was only waiting."

They stood side by side and watched the light pass slowly over the sea and come back. Oleg suddenly realized that he could no longer hear his own thoughts, which had been loud and interrupting one another all these weeks. Up here they had gone quiet.

He remembered the envelope, went down to his room and opened it. Inside lay a single sheet in his mother's handwriting. "I wanted you to see how a light is lit," it said. "Dad once lit it for me. Now it is your turn to decide what you want to shine on." Nothing more.

Oleg read the sheet twice and climbed back up. The beam still reached out into the dark.

"A good letter?" asked Tamara.

"A good one," he said. "I think I know what to answer."

In the morning he would write to his mother. For now he stood at the window and counted the flashes until he lost count. The lighthouse shone on Quiet Point, and that seemed to be enough.
